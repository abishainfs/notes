# people, labour and systems

there is a version of design thinking that begins and ends with the object, this is not that

---

## each object hides a network

a piece of furniture that looks like one object may have passed through ten or twenty people's hands before it reaches a room.

designer. drafter. carpenter. metalworker. polisher. stoneworker. upholsterer. packer. driver. installer. client. accounts.

the finished object hides the system that produced it. and the cleaner the object looks, the easier it is to forget how many people were involved in making it. this is not a criticism, but it's just something worth remembering when we talk about what a design choice costs, and who actually carries that cost

and most often design effort is the most expendable cost, as we are often blind to the cost that we ourselves carry, to the organisation!

## people are not workshop resources

when you're working with contractors and vendors, it becomes very easy to think in terms of capacity, lead times and output.

a carpenter becomes "three days". a polisher becomes "available on thursday". a vendor becomes "the person who does the bending".

but every one of those people has a life outside the production schedule.

this doesn't mean deadlines stop mattering. it just changes how you communicate them. and maybe how you react when something doesn't go to plan.

## visibility is unevenly distributed

the person whose name appears in the catalogue may have spent ten minutes on an object.

the person who actually made it may never be named.

that doesn't automatically make the design dishonest. but it does make me think more carefully about where recognition goes, and why.

## communication is infrastructure

emails, quotations, whatsapp messages, drawings, follow ups. they can all feel like things around the design.

they aren't.

they determine how information moves between the client, designer, workshop and business.

a bad email doesn't just stay a bad email. it can become the wrong dimension, the wrong finish, a missed deadline, rework, wasted material or extra cost.

good communication is load bearing.

## trust is part of the product

with bespoke furniture, the client is often paying for something they haven't seen yet.

so they aren't just buying the object.

they are buying some amount of trust in:

- the drawing, that it represents what will actually be made
    
- the material, that it will look and behave roughly as described
    
- the timeline, that the date given means something
    
- the maker, that the craft will be there
    
- the designer, that the decisions have been thought through
    
- the organisation, that someone will take responsibility when something goes wrong
    

trust isn't really a soft thing. it has very practical consequences.

when trust breaks, the object can feel wrong even when the object itself is technically fine.

## the quotation is a design document

a quotation isn't just a price.

it tells the client:

- what they're getting
    
- what they're not getting
    
- what material is being used
    
- what might change
    
- what is still uncertain
    
- what happens next
    

i've come to think that clarity in a quotation is part of the service itself.

a vague quotation pushes uncertainty onto the client without really telling them that it is there.

a precise one is a form of honesty.

## where this connects to service design

the hidden network. communication. trust. the workshop. the client. the vendor.

all of these are service design problems before they are furniture problems.

i think this is probably one of the reasons service design started making sense to me.

casegoods made me realise that the object is only one part of the experience around it.

[[Response_Able Futures]] will probably push this thinking much further. especially the question of who a service is designed _with_ and who it is designed _for_.
