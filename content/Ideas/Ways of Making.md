in some ways, the relationship between finn juhl and niels vodder reminds me a lot of our time working with devilal. there was a certain directness in the process which is very difficult to explain unless you've worked like that. details could be figured out standing right there at the machine, proportions changed midway, difficult curves or strange transitions solved almost instinctively. sometimes kenneth would sketch something and by evening deviji would already have some rough mockup or solution worked out in solid wood. there was very little seperation between drawing and making.

he understood the intricacies of the work very deeply, especially complicated joineries, curved members, weird alignments, all these things. things like the drift chair back transition, or the way the fillet joint edge table edge was resolved, these were things he could understand immedietely without too much explaining. turnaround was incredibly fast because of this. many times things would just happen in the kaarkhaney itself while discussing. one person holding the piece, one person randha maaro-ing one edge, another fellow standing and arguing about proportion. 

at the same time, it was also a very intense process. he was extremely single-minded in his way of working, very stubborn, and honestly sometimes quite difficult to handle. once he had decided something about how a thing should be made, it was very hard to move him from that position. there was a lot of friction also, but strangely that friction itself produced good work. many times the process itself felt slightly unstable but somehow the final object would come out with a lot of clarity.

working with jeevaramji and punaram feels very different. the process is less like working with one master craftsman and more like working with a whole workshop ecosystem that has developed over years. in some ways it reminds me more of wegner and johannes hansen. not one singular artisan relationship, but a larger workshop structure with systems, timelines, movement of materials, gaadi logistics between bombay and alibaug, multiple carpenters, polish people, machine operators etc all involved.

because they are based in alibaug also, the rhythm of the process changes. things need more planning beforehand. drawings need to be clearer. sometimes you cannot just stand there and resolve something instantly because material has already moved, or the carpenter working on it that day is someone else entirely. but at the same time, there is stability in that system. scale also becomes possible in a different way. the workshop itself carries knowledge now, not just one person.

both systems produce meaningful work, but through very different kinds of proximity and negotiation between design and making. one feels like an extention of one craftsman’s mind. the other feels like entering into an already existing institution of making and learning how to work with its own rhythm.
