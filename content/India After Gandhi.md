### 13. Tribal Trouble

> [!hl-blue] [p. 351](https://play.google.com/books/reader?id=ZbWIPAAAAEAJ&pg=GBS.PA351.w.0.0.0.1) · 7 Jun 2026
> Phizo joined the Japanese on their march to India, apparently in return for the promise of Naga independence should they succeed in winning their war against the British.
>
> **Note:** The Japanese advance into India during World War II was part of a broader campaign to defeat the British in Asia and potentially trigger the collapse of British rule in India. By 1942, Japan had already conquered: Malaya Singapore Burma Once Burma fell, the border with India became the next frontier. The Japanese Plan In 1944, Japan launched the U-Go Offensive. The objectives were: 1. Capture the strategic towns of Imphal and Kohima. 2. Disrupt Allied supply routes into China. 3. Encourage anti-British uprisings within India. 4. Potentially march deeper into eastern India if the offensive succeeded. Japan wasn't planning a full occupation of all India in the immediate term. Rather, they hoped a major victory would destabilize British control and create political opportunities. The Indian National Army Connection This is where things get complicated. The Subhas Chandra Bose-led Indian National Army fought alongside Japanese forces. Bose believed: > Britain's enemies could become India's temporary allies if it helped secure independence. Many Indians disagreed with this strategy, especially given Japan's record in Asia, but Bose saw it as a pragmatic wartime alliance. Why Phizo Was Interested The Naga leader Angami Zapu Phizo wasn't primarily concerned with Indian independence. He was concerned with Naga independence. Like many nationalist leaders in colonized regions, he saw the war as an opportunity: Britain seemed vulnerable. Japan appeared ascendant. Promises of self-determination were being made. So he apparently hoped Japanese success might create an independent Naga homeland. What Happened? The Japanese advance was stopped at the twin battles of: Battle of Imphal Battle of Kohima These battles are often called the "Stalingrad of the East." The Japanese suffered catastrophic losses from: combat, starvation, disease, overstretched supply lines. After their defeat, Japan never seriously threatened India again. A Small Historical Irony Many Nagas actually fought against the Japanese alongside British and Indian troops. Naga villagers served as guides, scouts, porters, and intelligence sources for the Allies during the Battle of Kohima. So while Phizo saw the Japanese as a potential route to Naga independence, many other Nagas were helping defeat them. That's one reason the politics of Nagaland after independence became so complex: there was never a single unified Naga position during the war.
