"The field of human-computer interaction (HCI) emerged in the

early eighties partly as a response to these changing conditions. It

was linked to, but somewhat distinct from, its human factors genitor in both its eclectic makeup and its more theoretical bias.3

The older field, somewhat dismissively referred to as "knobs and

dials" psychology, was seen as lacking in theoretical motivation by

cognitive scientists. What was required was a better _cognitive_

_coupling_ between the human and the new universal machine, the

computer, and not simply better designed surface characteristics of

displays."

Bannon's text was published in the book _Design at Work: Cooperative Design of Computer Systems_ (1991), edited by Joan Greenbaum and Morten Kyng. He was surrounded by peers writing in a similar vein:

- **The Scandinavian Participatory Design Movement:** In Northern Europe, researchers and labor unions were already arguing that workers should actively co-design the technology entering their workplaces, rather than having it imposed on them by management.
- **Don Norman's User-Centered Design:** In 1986, Donald Norman and Stephen Draper published _User Centered System Design_, followed by Norman’s seminal 1988 book _The Psychology of Everyday Things_ (later renamed _The Design of Everyday Things_). Norman was heavily pushing the rhetoric of shifting the burden of technology from the person to the machine. - **Lucy Suchman’s Situated Action:** In 1987, Lucy Suchman published _Plans and Situated Actions_, which fundamentally challenged artificial intelligence and cognitive science by arguing that human actions are strictly determined by immediate, unfolding social and physical situations, not rigid internal scripts.
