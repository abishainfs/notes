# The Positivist Approach in Research

> **REPORT**  
> *Sunday, September 27, 2026*

Positivism holds that genuine knowledge comes only from observable, measurable facts studied through the methods of natural science. Applied to social and human research, it assumes reality is objective and external, that the researcher stays detached from what is studied, and that the goal is to test hypotheses, find causal laws, and produce results that generalize. In practice it favors quantitative data, structured measurement, and statistical analysis.

## I. Philosophical Foundations

| | Position | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Ontology** | **REALIST** | One objective reality exists, independent of the observer. Social phenomena are treated as real, external things that can be measured much like physical objects. |
| **2. Epistemology** | **OBJECTIVIST** | Knowledge is valid only when grounded in observable, verifiable evidence. Claims that cannot be empirically tested - values, feelings, metaphysics - fall outside the domain of science. |
| **3. Researcher role** | **DETACHED** | The researcher is a neutral, value-free observer. Personal bias is minimized through standardized instruments and controlled procedures so findings reflect the world, not the investigator. |

## II. Core Principles

| Principle | Description |
|---|---|
| **Empiricism** | Only what can be observed and measured counts as evidence. Data comes through the senses and instruments, not intuition or speculation. |
| **Deductive logic** | Research starts from theory, derives hypotheses, then tests them against data. Confirmation or refutation feeds back into the theory. |
| **Causality & laws** | The aim is to explain phenomena through cause-and-effect relationships and to formulate general laws that hold across cases. |
| **Generalization** | Findings from a representative sample are expected to apply to the wider population, supporting prediction and replication. |
| **Value freedom** | Science should be separated from the researcher's beliefs and politics. Objectivity is protected through standardized, repeatable methods. |

## III. Methodology in Practice

| Area | Description |
|---|---|
| **Typical designs** | Quantitative and structured. Positivist studies lean on methods that yield numeric, comparable data.<br><br>- Experiments and quasi-experiments with control and treatment groups.<br>- Large-scale surveys and structured questionnaires.<br>- Structured observation and existing statistical datasets.<br>- Statistical analysis to test hypotheses and measure significance. |
| **Quality criteria** | Validity, reliability, objectivity. A good study measures what it claims to, produces consistent results on repetition, and remains independent of who runs it. |

## IV. Positivism vs. Interpretivism

**The main contrast**

| | Positivism | Interpretivism |
|---|---|---|
| **Reality** | Single, objective | Multiple, socially constructed |
| **Goal** | Explain, predict | Understand meaning |
| **Logic** | Deductive | Inductive |
| **Data** | Quantitative | Qualitative |
| **Researcher** | Detached, neutral | Involved, reflexive |
| **Output** | Generalizable laws | Contextual insight |

## V. Positivism vs. Feminist Approaches

**The core disagreement**

Feminist research rejects the idea of a detached, value-free observer as itself a disguised standpoint. Where positivism treats objectivity as neutrality, feminist scholars argue that the “view from nowhere” has historically encoded a male, powerful perspective as universal. Knowledge is instead situated - partial, located, and shaped by who is asking.

| | Positivism | Feminist approach |
|---|---|---|
| **Objectivity** | Value-free, neutral | Situated; neutrality masks a gendered default |
| **Purpose** | Explain, predict, generalize | Emancipate; expose and challenge gender inequality |
| **Researcher role** | Detached observer | Reflexive, involved, aware of power |
| **Relationship** | Hierarchical; subject as data source | Collaborative, reciprocal, non-hierarchical |
| **Data** | Quantitative, standardized | Often qualitative; centers women's lived experience |
| **Values** | Excluded from science | Acknowledged and embraced |

### Judith Stacey's caution

*Reflexive turn*

Stacey complicates the feminist ideal from the inside. In her 1988 essay *“Can There Be a Feminist Ethnography?”* she argues that the intimacy, rapport, and reciprocity feminist fieldwork prizes do not dissolve the power imbalance positivism ignores - they can deepen the risk of exploitation and betrayal.

- The closeness that makes feminist research ethical also leaves participants more exposed to manipulation.
- The researcher still controls the interpretation, the authorship, and the final text - an inescapable inequality.
- Feminist method is therefore more honest about power than positivism, not free of it; its virtue is reflexivity, not a clean escape.

> “The lives, loves, and tragedies that fieldwork informants share with a researcher are ultimately data - grist for the ethnographic mill, a mill that has a truly grinding power.”
>
> — Judith Stacey, 1988

## VI. Strengths and Criticisms

### Strengths — FOR

- Objective, replicable procedures reduce personal bias.
- Large samples support generalization and prediction.
- Numeric data allows precise comparison and statistical testing.
- Transparent methods make studies easy to scrutinize and repeat.

### Criticisms — AGAINST

- Reduces complex human experience to numbers, missing meaning and context.
- Treating people like physical objects ignores agency and subjectivity.
- Full value freedom is arguably impossible; choices of topic and measure carry bias.
- Rigid designs can strip behavior of its natural social setting.

## VII. Origins & Note on Post-Positivism

### Where it comes from

Rooted in Enlightenment empiricism and named by Auguste Comte in the 19th century, positivism sought to extend the success of natural science to the study of society.

Émile Durkheim's rule to treat “social facts as things” is a classic application.

Modern post-positivism softens the original claims: it accepts that observation is theory-laden and that knowledge is probabilistic and fallible, so researchers aim to approximate objective truth and falsify theories rather than prove them with certainty.
